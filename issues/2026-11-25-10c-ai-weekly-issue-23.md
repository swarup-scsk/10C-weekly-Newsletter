## Headline of the week

Model supply has been a market of privately held vendors with no published financials. That changed this week for one of the two largest suppliers. On 19 November 2026, Anthropic's shares began trading, and CNET covered the listing live on the day ([CNET, 19 November 2026](https://www.cnet.com/tech/services-and-software/openai-chatgpt-astra-6-2-microsoft-ignite-news)). The listing itself and the date are confirmed. The $81.3 billion raise figure and the "largest IPO in history" description that circulated afterwards appear only in an aggregator blog and could not be confirmed from a filing, a prospectus or a wire report, so we are not publishing that number ([AISA, 22 November 2026](https://aisa.to/blog/latest-ai-news-week-47-2026)).

**[Anthropic IPO Live: The Claude Maker Goes Public Today](https://www.cnet.com/tech/services-and-software/openai-chatgpt-astra-6-2-microsoft-ignite-news)**
*CNET's live coverage of the listing on 19 November 2026, the day Anthropic's shares began trading.* ⚠️

10C take: A listed supplier answers to shareholders and reports every quarter, which changes the rhythm of price changes, product decisions and roadmap commitments that clients depend on. Any client that has standardised on Claude should now be asking what its contract says about price changes, model deprecation and service continuity, because those terms matter more when the supplier has public growth targets. This is a procurement conversation, not a technical one, and it is best had before the next renewal rather than at it.

## The Briefs

**Models and tools**

At Ignite in San Francisco on 17 November, Microsoft announced in-house MAI models covering software development, cyber defence, image work, audio and decision tasks. The company said its MAI Code 2 model reached 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's performance across four coding benchmarks at 5% of the price, on a promotional rate ([eWeek, 19 November 2026](https://www.eweek.com/news/microsoft-seven-ai-models-openai-anthropic)). ⚠️ The count is not consistent across outlets: eWeek reports seven models, while [another report counts six](https://shattered.io/microsoft-unveils-six-mai-models-ignite-code-2-98-percent-claim) yet names eight distinct products in the same piece. eWeek's own caveat is the part worth carrying into a business case: a cheaper coding model can cost more once retries, review time and post-promotion pricing are included.

Why it matters to us: model sourcing is no longer a two-horse choice, and the benchmark number is the least reliable input to the decision. The useful test is the client's own backlog, run at the client's own review standards, with the price assumptions held at post-promotion levels.

## Client lens

For manufacturing, energy and logistics clients, the practical question is usually about embedded models inside operational systems rather than models chosen directly. A second credible supplier at a much lower headline price gives those clients real negotiating room with their platform vendors, but only if someone tests the cheaper model on the actual work: exception handling, legacy code, poor-quality input documents. A benchmark run on clean public problems tells you very little about a maintenance backlog or a freight document set.

For healthcare and financial services, the listing is the more consequential item. These clients already have to explain and evidence why a particular system was chosen, and a supplier with public quarterly reporting gives them a new set of things to monitor: pricing changes, model retirements, changes of strategic direction. The engagement opportunity is a short, concrete piece of work: a supplier register that records which models sit inside which systems, what the contract says about change, and what switching would involve. That is a few weeks of effort and it answers a question clients will be asked eventually.

## Conversation Starter

A point you can raise in a client conversation this week:

> "Two questions on your model strategy. If your main model supplier were now a listed company with quarterly targets, what in your contract would you want to change? And have you tested the cheaper alternative on your own backlog, or only on the vendor's benchmark?"

This opens a useful conversation because most clients have an answer to the second question and no answer at all to the first.
